Key theoretical contribution CEL, new draft

The ledger is the log-odds of error. Draws do not move the odds that a stable answer is wrong. Each probe multiplies them by its likelihood ratio.

It lifts Lemma 4 from a test to the delivered set, and it explains the allocation (probes before draws) and the residual model (η for stability, β for what draws cannot see). Formal statement and proofs: docs/paper/sec_separation.tex; plain-language note and tests: docs/separation_theorem.md.

The theorem in plain words

Imagine 100 questions where the model gives the same answer every time you ask. That looks reliable, but it isn't always right. Say 80 of the answers are right. 15 are wrong because the model reacts to a surface pattern in the wording (a "cue-follower"). 5 are wrong because the model truly believes a false fact (a "false belief").

What the theorem says

  1. Asking again does not help. Paraphrase the question and ask 10 more times. All 100 still give the same answer, so you still cannot tell the 20 wrong ones from the 80 right ones. Pick any 10 and, on average, 2 are wrong. This is true however many times you ask.
  2. A trick question does help. Ask a version that should flip the answer, for example "Is it not true that …?" A model that understands flips its answer correctly (95% of the time). A cue-follower sees the same pattern and gets it right only by luck (50%). So each trick question removes about half of the cue-followers and keeps almost all the right answers.
  3. Some errors survive every trick. A model with a false belief applies that belief consistently, so it also flips its answer "correctly". No trick question removes these 5. They set a floor.
  4. Too many tricks start to hurt. Each trick also loses a few right answers (5% each time), while the false beliefs always stay. After about 5 tricks, the losses outweigh the gains.

Why it is true (the proof, without symbols)

  1. Count the evidence as odds. Before any trick question, the odds that a consistent answer is wrong are 20 to 80, or 1 to 4.
    \[ O_0 = \frac{15 + 5}{80} = \frac{1}{4} \]
  2. Asking again gives no evidence about right versus wrong. Right answers and wrong-but-consistent answers repeat in exactly the same way. A clue that looks the same under both explanations cannot change the odds, so it multiplies them by 1.
    \[ \frac{\Pr(D \mid \text{wrong})}{\Pr(D \mid \text{right})} = 1 \]
  3. A trick question gives evidence. Passing it is about half as likely for a cue-follower as for a right answer. So passing multiplies the cue-followers' share of the odds by about one half (0.5 / 0.95). For a false belief the factor is about 1 (1 / 0.95), so that share stays.
    \[ \lambda_{\mathrm F} = \frac{0.5}{0.95} \approx 0.53, \qquad \lambda_{\mathrm B} = \frac{1}{0.95} \approx 1.05 \]
  4. Independent clues multiply. If the trick questions don't share a weakness, their factors multiply. Taking logarithms turns the multiplication into addition. That running sum is exactly what CEL's ledger keeps. This is the theorem: the ledger is the log-odds that the answer is wrong.
    \[ O_r = \frac{15}{80}\,(0.53)^r + \frac{5}{80}\,(1.05)^r, \qquad \log O_r = \log O_0 + \sum_{j} \log \lambda_j \ \text{ (one wrong type)} \]
  5. The rest follows by reading the formula. Asking again multiplies by 1, so it never moves the odds. Each trick multiplies by about one half for cue-followers, so their share shrinks fast. The false beliefs' factor never falls below 1, so they remain as a floor.

The price of a guarantee

To promise "at most α of the delivered answers are wrong", CEL compares each new answer with a set of Mcal answers that were checked by hand. A promise can never be finer than that reference set. With 100 checked answers and 10 delivered out of 50, no promise can go below about 5%, however good the scores are. With 200 it is 2.5%. Better scores bring the promise closer to that limit. Worse scores do not break the promise: CEL then delivers fewer answers.

The plain story assumes trick questions don't share a weakness and that the reason for an error doesn't depend on how stable the answer is. Both can be checked on labelled data. Formal version: docs/paper/sec_separation.tex.

The method at a glance

Calibrate once on a small labelled set. Then use one of two modes. The ranking mode fixes the budget: for each batch, spend the calls on the cases at the top-k cut (the frontier rule), score each case pessimistically, and deliver the top k, optionally only those that pass the conformal step. The certified mode fixes the guarantee: draw until a case reaches the certification depth, or stop at the first disagreement. Dashed boxes and arrows are proposals that are not in the code yet; everything else is in the code (decisions.md #17–19).

The methodology, step by step

Written in ASD-STE100 style. Each step names the theory behind it. Flags: CEL a result CEL contributes (in the paper draft); CEL, new a result from the redesign, not in the paper yet; no flag: a classical or borrowed result, with its source. exact and model-based show the type of each result.

Terms

  • A case is one question and one candidate answer.
  • A call is one request to the model under test.
  • A draw is one answer to one fresh paraphrase of the question.
  • Z is correctness: Z = 1 if the answer is correct.
  • Stability p̄ is the probability that one draw agrees with the case's answer.
  • The budget k is the maximum mean number of calls for each case.

Part 0: The objective

  1. From each batch of 50 cases, select the k cases that are most probably correct.
    Theory: Proposition 1, optimality of top-k selection CEL exact. If accuracy increases with the score (Assumption 1, Monotonicity), the top k by score give the most correct cases in expectation. The proof uses an exchange argument and needs no independence between cases. Assumption 1 is tested on the data (monotonicity table).

Part A: Calibration (do one time for each dataset and model)

  1. Select M cases, for example M = 200. Do not use cases from the eval set.
    Theory: exchangeability of calibration and test cases. The conformal guarantee (Part B, step 7) needs it. Nothing is tuned on eval items. Three rules set the minimum M: (1) conformal: Mcal + 1 ≥ m/(αk), so Mcal ≥ 49 for m = 50, k = 10, α = 0.1; (2) regression: about 5–10 wrong answers per signal in the fit cases, so with 8 signals and 80% accuracy, Mfit ≈ 200–400; (3) prior: 5 draws per case on about 200 cases gave a depth within ±2 draws in simulation. Cost: 8M calls per dataset × model.
  2. For each case, generate fresh paraphrases (1 call). Use one paraphrase for each draw.
    Theory: Lemma 1, fresh-paraphrase i.i.d. sampling CEL exact. A mixture of Bernoullis is a Bernoulli, so the draws are i.i.d. with mean p̄. Lemma 2, design effect (Kish 1965, applied by CEL) exact: reuse of a paraphrase multiplies the variance by 1 + (m − 1)ρ.
  3. Get 5 draws and 2 probes (negation, relabel) for each case: 8 calls in total. Record the 8 signals.
    Theory: Lemma 4, power separation CEL exact. A model that follows a surface cue gives the same draws as a correct model with the same p̄. Thus draws cannot find it. A probe that inverts the cue can find it.
  4. Fit a prior Beta(a, b) to the agreement counts. Bootstrap the fit and keep the 95th percentile of the certification depth.
    Theory: empirical Bayes with a beta-binomial model (Robbins 1956) model-based. The pessimistic bootstrap depth CEL, new protects against a prior that certifies too early. In code (stability_prior.py): 5 fresh draws of every Dfit and Dcal case, labels unread; 1,000 resamples; replayed on eval next to the Jeffreys depth.
    Step 4 in detail

    A certificate needs the posterior ε = P(p̄ < 0.8 | the draws), and a posterior needs a prior on p̄. The Jeffreys prior assumes nothing about the cell, so it needs 9 unanimous draws. In a real cell most answers are very stable. This step learns that from the calibration cases, without labels, so that a case needs fewer draws. The figure is one simulated cell (M = 200, true prior Beta(8, 1)). It shows the procedure, not data.

    1. Data. Each calibration case i has mi agreeing draws out of n = 5. Most cases agree 5/5, and a few agree 3/5 or 4/5. The histogram of mi is the only input. It needs no labels, because agreement is not correctness.
    2. Fit the prior. Model each case's stability as p̄i ~ Beta(a, b), with mi | p̄i ~ Binomial(5, p̄i). Then mi is beta-binomial. Choose (a, b) to maximise Σi [ln B(a + mi, b + 5 − mi) − ln B(a, b)]. This is empirical Bayes: the population of cases sets the prior for any one case. Check the fit: the blue dots (expected counts) must match the bars.
    3. Read off the depth. After d unanimous draws, the posterior is Beta(a + d, b), and ε(d) is its mass below 0.8. The depth is the first d with ε(d) ≤ 0.05. Here the fitted prior already puts only 14% of its mass below 0.8, so ε starts low and crosses 0.05 at d = 6. Jeffreys starts at 0.70 and needs d = 9.
    4. Bootstrap, and be pessimistic. The fit uses only 200 cases, so (a, b) are noisy. A fit that is too optimistic makes the depth too small, and the certificate then claims ε ≤ 0.05 when the real tail mass is larger. To protect against this, resample the 200 cases with replacement 1,000 times, refit, and record the depth each time. Use the 95th percentile (here 8), not the point estimate (6). In this run, 11% of the resamples gave a prior so optimistic that it certified with 0 draws. The pessimistic rule protects against exactly this.

    What you get: one depth per dataset × model, between the true value and the Jeffreys value: 8 here, against 6 (true) and 9 (Jeffreys). The saving is largest in cells with very stable answers. A prior with mean near 0.8 saves nothing, and it can need more draws than Jeffreys (see the prior table in the method notes). Warning: the certificate is then model-based. It holds only if the Beta family describes the cell, and it is only about stability, not correctness.

  5. Split the M cases: Mfit to fit, Mcal to calibrate (for example, 100 and 100).
    Theory: split conformal (Vovk et al. 2005). The score must not see the calibration cases. In code: Mfit is the dev slice (split.n_dev, 100) and Mcal is split.n_cal (200), drawn after the eval items from one shuffle and collected in full, not as the 8-call battery.
  6. For each fit case, calculate the stability term η = E[logit p̄ | draws] = ψ(a + m) − ψ(b + n − m), with the cell prior Beta(a, b) (ψ is the digamma function).
    Theory: exact moment of the logit of a Beta variable. η carries what the draws say about stability, with the prior of step 4.
  7. On the Mfit fit cases, fit a logistic regression of Z on η and the other signals: logit P(Z = 1) = a0 + a1·η + Σ βg xg. Put the ridge penalty on β only, not on a0 and a1. Record the coefficients and their covariance.
    Theory: logistic regression with an unpenalised stability term (classical GLM). The β terms model the residual: what correctness does beyond stability (Lemma 4: stable-but-wrong cases). The penalty shrinks toward the stability-only model, so β = 0 means “trust stability”. a0 and a1 stay free because accuracy is not equal to stability. The target stays Z, never stability. The ratio βg/a1 is the empirical exchange rate κ̂ (Proposition 3).
  8. Calculate the SHAP value of each term for each fit case: φg(x) = βg·(xg − mean xg), and a1·(η − mean η) for stability. The β terms show importance beyond stability.
    Theory: SHAP (Lundberg & Lee 2017). For a model that is linear in the log-odds, SHAP has this closed form (interventional, features treated as independent). No refits and no calls.
  9. Rank the signals by importance: Ig = mean |φg|. Bootstrap the fit cases to get an interval for each rank.
    Theory: global SHAP importance. Unlike β alone, Ig counts how often a signal changes: a probe that nearly always passes has a small Ig, even with a large β. For a pass/fail signal with pass rate π, Ig = |βg|·2π(1 − π).
  10. Remove the free signals with a small, unstable importance. Refit.
    Theory: fewer signals give more wrong answers per signal, so the fit is more stable at a given Mfit.
  11. Calculate the value of each paid call type: vg = Ig/cg, where cg is its calls. Sort the paid call types by value. In code (signal_value.py, steps 8–11): importance with every signal bought on Dfit, a bootstrap 90% interval, free signals pruned below 0.01, a draw valued at Iη per draw. The resulting knapsack plan is reported next to the frontier rule (B2) and the enumerated plan.
    Theory: Propositions K and K′ CEL, new model-based. All calls cost the same, so the allocation is a knapsack with equal weights. The greedy order by value is optimal. Background: Chernoff's sequential design (1959) and the oracle weights of Track-and-Stop (Garivier & Kaufmann 2016) give the same order for one binary question. Check: compare the order with κ (Prop. 3) and with the forward marginal gains on the fit cases. A difference shows double-counting or redundancy.
  12. Select the pessimism weight λ by cross-validation on the Mfit cases. Then freeze β, Cov(β̂), the signal set, the values, λ and the prior.
    Procedure: (1) split the Mfit cases into 5 folds; (2) for each fold, fit on the other 4 and replay the deployment policy at budget k on the held-out fold, so each case gets only the calls it would get and its σ̂ comes from the matching sub-model; (3) for each λ in {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3}, draw 1,000 random batches of m cases from the held-out predictions and average prec@k; (4) keep the λ with the highest mean; if the curve is flat within its bootstrap interval, keep the smaller λ. Never use the Mcal cases (the conformal guarantee needs a score that has not seen them) or the eval cases. λ is in standard errors of the log-odds: λ = 1.645 is a one-sided 95% lower bound for each case, and λ = z1−γ/m (2.88 for m = 50, γ = 0.1) is a bound for all m cases at once. These are the reference points if the data are too few to choose. In code: 5-fold CV on Mfit, grid {0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3}, criterion = precision of the top k/m share of the pooled held-out cases, ties to the smaller λ. For the frontier, each fit case gives 8 random partial states (draws 1..all, each probe and the challenge with probability ½), with all states of one case in one fold.

Part B: Ranking mode (do for each batch)

  1. For each case, generate fresh paraphrases (1 call).
    In code: paraphrased or original-wording draws are chosen per budget on the dev batches; at 2–3 calls the generator call is often not worth it.
  2. Spend the calls by the frontier rule. Give each case 1 draw. Then, one call at a time, until the batch has spent k calls per case on average:
    1. Rank the cases by μ̂. Among the current top k, find the case with the lowest μ̂ − σ̂. Among the others, find the case with the highest μ̂ + σ̂. Select the one of the two with the larger σ̂.
    2. For that case, buy the call with the largest expected move of μ̂: for each available call (next draw, each flip probe, the challenge), take the pass probability μ̂·q₁ + (1 − μ̂)·q̂₀ from Mfit, and average |μ̂ after pass − μ̂| and |μ̂ after fail − μ̂|.
    Theory: LUCB for top-k identification (Kalyanakrishnan et al. 2012), here on the model-based μ̂ ± σ̂, so model-based with no guarantee. Calls end up uneven by design: cases far from the cut keep 1 draw, cases at the cut get up to every call. This is the setting where the pessimistic score can change the top k (step B5). The knapsack order of Proposition K CEL, new is the per-case alternative (A11, in code); the report sets the two side by side. frontier.py, decisions.md #18.
  3. Calculate the signals of each case.
  4. Calculate the estimate μ̂ = xᵀβ̂ and its standard error σ̂ from three terms. Here x = (1, η, x1, …, xG). For a signal that was not bought, put its calibration mean in x.
    σ̂i² = xᵀ Cov(β̂) x (fit uncertainty) + a1²·Var(ηi) (stability uncertainty) + Σg not bought βg²·Var(xg) (missing-signal uncertainty)
    • Fit term. The delta method on the ridge fit, Cov(β̂) = (XᵀŴX + Λ)⁻¹ (classical). It comes from the Mfit cases only and is almost the same for every case.
    • Stability term. Var(ηi) = ψ′(a + m) + ψ′(b + n − m), with ψ′ the trigamma function. This is the exact posterior variance of logit p̄ given the draws of this case. It falls as draws are added.
    • Missing-signal term. For an unbought pass/fail probe with calibration pass rate πg, Var(xg) = πg(1 − πg). The term becomes 0 when the probe is bought.

    In code (score_model.py): all three terms. Pass/fail signals are coded ±1, so their variance is 4π(1 − π); means and variances come from Mfit. The prior in η is Jeffreys (½, ½) until the fitted prior of A4 exists.

    Why three terms: with the fit term alone, an unbought signal set to its centred mean (0) drops out of xᵀCov(β̂)x. Then a case with fewer calls gets a smaller σ̂ and is ranked less cautiously, the opposite of the intent. The last two terms make σ̂ depend on what each case bought: a case with 2 draws and no probes has a wide interval, and each call narrows it. Assumption: the signals are treated as independent given the fit (covariances between xg are ignored).

  5. Calculate the score s = μ̂ − λ·σ̂.
    Theory: the pessimism principle: select by a lower confidence bound (Jin, Yang & Wang 2021; used for Best-of-N by Yu, Wu & Block 2026). It corrects the winner's curse of top-k selection. It is a heuristic: it gives no guarantee.

    What is new, and what is not. μ̂, the delta method and the LCB rule are all classical. They are not a contribution. The possible contributions CEL, new are: (1) the per-case variance decomposition above, which makes the pessimism depend on the budget of each case; (2) one decomposition that drives both the score and the allocation: the value of the next call for case i is the expected drop in σ̂i, which matters most near the top-k boundary; (3) open a selection result for top-k with unequal noise per case, for example under a Gaussian model: an LCB with case-specific variance bounds the winner's-curse bias of the selected set, and the plain-mean ranking does not. Without (3), present B5 as engineering and put the paper's weight on the evidence-odds theorem.

  6. Sort the cases by s. Deliver the top k.
    Theory: Proposition 1 CEL.
  7. Optional: calculate a conformal p-value for each case against the Mcal calibration cases. Apply Benjamini–Hochberg at level α. Deliver only the top-k cases that it selects. In code (certified_selection.py): on the fixed per-case allocation of CEL-k, with the pessimistic score, the plain μ̂ and self-consistency agreement (the draws-only baseline); also e-BH on ledger e-values, labelled model-based.
    Theory: conformal selection (Jin & Candès 2023) with Benjamini–Hochberg (1995) exact. The expected share of wrong answers in the selected set is ≤ α at finite n, if the cases are exchangeable. The motivation, to constrain the decision and not a sufficient condition, is from Conformal Robustness Control (Hu et al. 2026).
Step 7 in detail
  1. Prepare the reference. For each Mcal case, record its score si and its label Zi. Keep the scores of the wrong cases (Zi = 0).
  2. Calculate a p-value for each test case j: pj = (1 + number of wrong calibration cases with si ≥ sj) / (Mcal + 1).
  3. Sort the m p-values of the batch from small to large: p(1) ≤ … ≤ p(m).
  4. Find the largest r with p(r) ≤ r·α/m. Call it R. If no r passes, R = 0.
  5. Select the R cases with the smallest p-values (Benjamini–Hochberg).
  6. If R ≥ k, deliver the top k of the selected cases. If R < k, deliver the R cases with the guarantee and, if necessary, the next cases by score marked “not guaranteed”.

Example (m = 50, Mcal = 100): six cases have no wrong calibration case above them (p = 1/101 = 0.0099), three have one (0.0198), two have two (0.0297), one has three (0.0396). At α = 0.1 the threshold is 0.002·r: r = 6 passes (0.0099 ≤ 0.012), r = 7 to 12 fail, so R = 6. At α = 0.2 the threshold is 0.004·r: r = 12 passes (0.0396 ≤ 0.048), so R ≥ 12 and the top 10 are delivered.

The guarantee: E[share of wrong answers in the BH set] ≤ α. It is an average over batches, so one batch can be above α. It needs: calibration and test cases from the same dataset and model; the same frozen policy, score and λ; calls for each case that depend on that case only. Caution: the theorem covers the whole BH set. Keeping only its top k (step 6, case R > k) is not covered by the theorem.

Warning: without step 7, the ranking mode has no guarantee.

Part C: Certified mode (do for each case that needs a certificate)

  1. Get 3 draws. If they do not agree, stop. Do not certify the case.
    Theory: Lemma 3, triage operating characteristics CEL exact. The screen passes a case with probability p̄³. It is the first stage of a truncated SPRT (Wald 1945).
  2. Get more draws until the case reaches the certification depth d.
    Theory: Corollary 1, certification depth CEL. With the Jeffreys prior, d = 9 exact. With the fitted prior, d comes from Part A, step 4 model-based. Both are in code and replayed on eval.
  3. If all draws agree, certify the case: ε ≤ 0.05 at p₀* = 0.8.
    Theory: Proposition 2, selection guarantee CEL. The expected number of delivered cases with p̄ < 0.8 is Σε. The proof uses linearity of expectation, so no independence between cases is necessary. It is exact given the prior.
  4. Optional: count passed probes as extra draws. Certify when the effective depth keff ≥ 8.35 and no probe fails.
    Theory: Proposition 3, evidence exchange CEL model-based. One passed negation or relabel probe is worth κ ≈ 2.1 draws; a passed distractor probe, κ ≈ 0.56. It assumes probes and draws are independent given the case's status.
    • Effective depth. keff = (agreeing draws) + Σ κg over passed probes; the fitted κ̂g = βg / a₁ (step A7) can replace the derived rate.
    • Where 8.35 comes from. With the Jeffreys prior, n unanimous draws give Beta(½ + n, ½). ε = P(p̄ < 0.8) reaches 0.05 at n = 8.35 when n is treated as continuous. Whole draws round this up to 9 (ε = 0.042). C4 compares keff with 8.35 (tail_mass_effective).
    • Example. With negation and relabel both passed (4.2 draws), 5 draws give keff = 9.2 (ε = 0.040): 7 calls in place of 9. Four draws give 8.2 (ε = 0.052), not enough. With one probe passed, 8.35 − 2.1 = 6.25 draws are needed, so 7: 8 calls. The saving is 1–2 calls per survivor, and the probes add evidence that plain draws never give (they can expose a cue-follower).
    • Why a failed probe blocks. The exchange only credits passes. A failed flip probe is strong evidence of a cue-follower (Lemma 4), and the rule has no matching debit, so any failure stops certification.
    • Limits. This certificate is model-based: it assumes probes and draws are independent given the case, and κ is itself a model quantity (derived from assumed pass rates, or estimated as κ̂), not an exact constant. Report it apart from the exact draws-only one. It also mixes two kinds of evidence. Probes separate correct answers from cue-followers, while the certificate is about stability, so it still says nothing about correctness. A consistent false belief passes every probe.
  5. Optional: use the GLR stopping rule in place of steps 2–3.
    Theory: GLR stopping of Track-and-Stop (Garivier & Kaufmann 2016) exact. P(certify | p̄ ≤ 0.8) ≤ δ for any order of calls. It needs about 25 unanimous draws at δ = 0.1. In code: the rule and its depths; it cannot be replayed on the current collection, which holds 12 fresh draws per case.

Part D: Shift between the modes

  1. Run the ranking mode. Keep all draws: they use fresh paraphrases, so they also count for the certificate.
  2. For each case that needs a certificate, get only the missing draws.

Two modes: CEL-k and certified CEL

Both modes read the same ledger of draws and probes. They differ in what you hold fixed. CEL-k fixes the budget and ranks: you get the k answers most likely to be correct, for at most k calls per case. Certified CEL fixes the evidence standard and lets the spend float: each answer gets more draws until the SPRT can certify or reject it, and you get every answer that meets the standard, each with its own certificate.

Three things that are easy to mix up

  1. "Certified" does not mean "the only mode with a guarantee". Conformal FDR control on correctness works for both modes and at any budget. What only certified CEL gives is a certificate on each delivered answer.
  2. The certificate is about stability, not correctness. It says the model would give this answer again on fresh paraphrases. A cue-following model can be stable and wrong; the probes and the conformal step are what reach correctness.
  3. CEL-k is not "certified CEL with fewer calls". Below about 9 agreeing draws (B*, Cor. 2) no plan can earn the exact certificate, so CEL-k at k = 2–5 never certifies an answer. It spends its calls on the plan that ranks best at that budget. As k grows, its curve runs into certified CEL's point (R4).

How to go from CEL-k to certified CEL on the same cases

  1. Run CEL-k with fresh paraphrases and deliver the top k.
  2. For each delivered case, count the agreeing draws you already have.
  3. Get more draws until the case reaches the certification depth (9), or until one draw disagrees.
  4. Certify the case if it reaches the depth; otherwise mark it "ranked, not certified".

Note: this costs only the missing draws. Warning: draws on the original wording are not independent (Lemma 1) and must not count toward the certificate.

What collection spends on one question

Collection records a superset: everything any method could ask for. The paraphrases and every flip (probe) are real calls, split across three model roles.

How one case is counted when methods are compared

At evaluation, each method replays the recorded calls it would have made and counts them: CEL pays for its paraphrases and its flips. Adaptive CEL certifies but costs 4–16 calls per case. CEL-k spreads a budget of k over a screen and the survivors' probes, chosen on the dev slice, so that its expected calls per case stay within k; self-consistency spends exactly k. The frontier variant spends exactly k·m calls per batch of m, unevenly: 1 call for a case far from the top-k cut, up to 20 for a case on it.

How a case is certified

A draw is one answer to one fresh paraphrase. Stability p̄ is the probability that a draw gives the case's answer. To certify a case is to state that its stability is probably at or above the standard p₀* = 0.8. The tail mass ε is the posterior probability that p̄ < 0.8. A case is certified when ε ≤ ε₀ = 0.05.

Warning: a certificate is about stability, not correctness. A model can give the same wrong answer every time, and that case can be certified. The probes exist to catch it.

Conformal step: a guarantee on correctness in code

The pessimistic score ranks cases but guarantees nothing. This step adds a guarantee on the share of wrong answers in the delivered set, with labels from the calibration cases only. The method is conformal selection (Jin & Candès, 2023). The paper's CRC idea motivates it: constrain the decision itself, not a sufficient condition such as "every case is certified".

StepAction
1Split the M calibration cases: Mfit to fit the score, Mcal to calibrate. Freeze the score after the fit.
2Run the same frozen policy (calls, signals, score s) on the Mcal calibration cases.
3For each test case j, calculate the conformal p-value: pj = (1 + #{calibration cases i with Zi = 0 and si ≥ sj}) / (Mcal + 1).
4Apply Benjamini–Hochberg at level α to the p-values of the batch.
5Deliver the top-k cases that BH selects. If BH selects fewer than k, deliver fewer, or mark the rest "not guaranteed".

The exact stability certificate (Prop. 2) stays as the label-free layer. The conformal guarantee is the label-calibrated layer on top. The split takes Mcal cases away from the regression; keep the signal count small or raise M.

GLR stopping rule in code depths only

The current SPRT tests two fixed points, p₀ = 0.7 against p₁ = 0.95. The generalized likelihood ratio (GLR) rule from Track-and-Stop tests the composite question "is p̄ above 0.8?" without a p₁. It stays valid for any order of calls, so it works with the greedy allocation.

StepAction

3If p̂ > 0.8 and Λ ≥ β(n, δ), stop and certify.
4If p̂ < 0.8 and Λ ≥ β(n, δ), stop and reject.
5Otherwise, buy the next call. Threshold used here: β(n, δ) = log(1/δ) + log n (the Track-and-Stop form with K = 1).

The GLR guarantee is frequentist and holds at every stability below 0.8, not only at 0.7, and under any stopping time. That strength costs draws: about 25 against 9. Tighter thresholds exist (mixture martingales), but the order of magnitude stays. Two uses fit the budget: the GLR rule as the stopping rule of the certified mode when a frequentist guarantee is required, and the GLR statistic as a free per-case signal in ranking mode. The 3-draw screen is a heuristic under GLR: one disagreement in 2 or 3 draws does not reach the reject threshold.
